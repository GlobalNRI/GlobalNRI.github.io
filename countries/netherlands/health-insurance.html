Health Insurance in the Netherlands: Expat Guide

Dutch health insurance is built around a compulsory standard package (basisverzekering) for people who are insured under the Dutch Healthcare Insurance Act. The right policy and start date depend on why you are in the Netherlands, whether you work here, and where you are socially insured. This guide focuses on practical decisions for newcomers and residents.

2026 quick facts: the statutory excess (eigen risico) for adults is €385 per calendar year; supplementary insurance is optional; and if you are required to insure under Dutch rules, do not wait for your BSN before contacting an insurer. Confirm your personal insurance position with the SVB or Zorgverzekeringslijn if you are a student, cross-border worker, posted worker or unsure where you are insured.

Who must take out Dutch basic insurance?

In general, people who live or work in the Netherlands must have Dutch basic health insurance. The rule is based on your insurance position under Dutch social-security law, not simply your visa, nationality or the fact that you have a Dutch address.

  • Employees working in the Netherlands: you will usually need Dutch basic insurance, including many international students who take a paid job. Ask the SVB if your situation is unusual.
  • People moving here without working: whether Dutch insurance applies depends on your residence and social-security circumstances. Check before relying on an overseas policy.
  • International students: studying alone does not always make you eligible for Dutch basic insurance. If you work, do an internship or receive certain benefits, the answer may change. Use the SVB Wlz assessment or contact Zorgverzekeringslijn.
  • Children under 18: they must be registered with an insurer, but pay no premium for the basic package and are not charged the adult statutory excess.
  • Visitors: short-stay visitors generally use travel or other applicable insurance; a visitor visa is not Dutch basic health insurance.

If you are required to take out Dutch insurance, arrange it within four months of the date the obligation starts. If you apply within that period, cover generally starts from the date you became insured under the Dutch rules, so premiums may be due for the earlier period too. Late enrollment can leave medical costs uncovered and may trigger follow-up by the CAK. Get advice promptly if you think the deadline has passed.

What the basic package covers

The government sets the standard package, and insurers must accept applicants for basic insurance regardless of age or health. The statutory package is broadly the same, but policies can differ in how they reimburse care from providers without a contract.

  • GP (huisarts) care and referrals to specialists.
  • Hospital treatment and most specialist care, subject to referral and policy conditions.
  • Many prescription medicines, mental-health care, maternity care and emergency care, subject to package rules and any applicable excess or personal contribution.
  • Some treatments are limited or excluded. Adult routine dental care, most glasses/contact lenses and much physiotherapy are not generally covered as standard, although exceptions apply.

Ask your insurer whether a referral is required, whether the provider has a contract, and what you would pay yourself. Emergency treatment and routine GP visits follow different cost rules.

Premium, excess and other out-of-pocket costs

Adults pay a monthly premium directly to the insurer. The 2026 statutory excess is €385 per adult per calendar year. It usually applies to covered care such as hospital treatment, specialist care and many medicines. It does not apply to GP visits, care for children under 18, or every type of care; check the insurer's explanation for the service in question.

  • You may choose an additional voluntary excess in exchange for a lower premium. This increases the amount you may have to pay yourself; only choose it if you can comfortably cover that risk.
  • A personal contribution (eigen bijdrage) is different from the excess and may apply to particular products or services.
  • Insurers set their own nominal premiums. They publish next year's premium and policy terms before the annual switching period; compare the actual quote for your chosen policy instead of relying on an old average.

Supplementary insurance: is it worth it?

Supplementary insurance (aanvullende verzekering) is optional. It can cover extra dental care, physiotherapy, glasses or other benefits, depending on the plan. Insurers may set health questions, acceptance conditions, annual limits, waiting periods or reimbursement caps for supplementary policies; they do not have the same acceptance duty as for basic insurance.

Estimate the care you realistically expect to use, compare the annual premium with the plan's maximum reimbursement, and read exclusions and waiting periods. A low monthly price does not necessarily mean a good value if the annual cap is small.

International students, expats and cross-border workers

  • Student with no Dutch job: you may need private/student insurance or coverage from your home country, depending on your circumstances. Confirm that the policy meets visa, school and healthcare needs.
  • Student with a Dutch job: Dutch basic insurance is often required once you are employed. Have your position assessed before the job starts; do not assume your student policy remains sufficient.
  • Posted worker or cross-border commuter: A1 certificates, country of employment and EU coordination rules can affect which country's system covers you. Check with your employer and the relevant social-security authority.
  • Non-working family members: their insurance position may differ from the working sponsor's. Confirm each family member separately.

For official help deciding whether you are insured under the Wlz, contact the Social Insurance Bank (SVB). For independent guidance in English, use Zorgverzekeringslijn.

How to choose a policy

  1. Check your eligibility and start date: confirm that Dutch basic insurance applies to you and when the obligation begins.
  2. Check hospitals and providers: search the insurer's contracted-care list for your preferred hospital, GP and pharmacy; compare reimbursement for non-contracted care.
  3. Compare the full cost: monthly premium, voluntary excess, personal contributions, reimbursement limits and payment frequency.
  4. Review service access: check English support, digital declarations, customer service hours and help finding a GP.
  5. Add extras only when useful: compare expected reimbursements with the extra premium, annual caps and acceptance conditions.
  6. Check healthcare benefit: if you are 18 or older and have Dutch insurance, you may qualify for zorgtoeslag depending on income, assets and other conditions. Apply through Dienst Toeslagen; eligibility is not automatic.

How to enroll after moving

  1. Confirm when your Dutch insurance obligation starts. If uncertain, ask SVB/Zorgverzekeringslijn before buying a policy that may not match your status.
  2. Choose an insurer and basic policy. You can usually apply online; the insurer may request identity, address, employment or social-security details.
  3. Apply within four months after the obligation starts. A BSN is useful and commonly requested, but do not let waiting for municipal registration cause you to miss the insurance deadline; contact the insurer about applying without it.
  4. Keep the policy confirmation and note the start date, premium, excess and insurer contact details.
  5. Register with a local GP separately. Being insured does not automatically register you with a GP, and practices may have local capacity limits.

Using healthcare once insured

  • For non-emergency concerns, start with a GP (huisarts), who can treat you or refer you to a specialist when appropriate.
  • Outside GP office hours, contact the regional GP out-of-hours service (huisartsenpost) for urgent problems that cannot wait. Call 112 for a life-threatening emergency.
  • Show your insurance details when asked, keep invoices and insurer messages, and check whether a referral or prior approval is needed before planned treatment.
  • Check claims and excess in your insurer's app or portal. The insurer may invoice the excess after it receives a provider's bill, not at the time you receive care.

Switching insurers: annual deadlines

You can normally change your basic insurer once a year. The annual policy terms and premiums are published before the new year. To switch for 1 January, cancel your current policy by 31 December and take out the new policy by 31 January. If you cancel the old policy yourself by 31 December, you have until 31 January to arrange the replacement; the new policy can then cover you retroactively from 1 January. Many insurers offer a transfer service if you apply with them by 31 December. Check the current government guidance and your policy terms, especially for supplementary cover, which may have separate acceptance rules.

Newcomer checklist

  • Confirm which country's social-security system covers you, especially if you are studying, posted by an employer or working across a border.
  • If Dutch basic insurance applies, arrange it within four months of the start date and budget for any retroactive premiums.
  • Compare contracted hospitals, medicines, excess and out-of-network reimbursement, not only the monthly premium.
  • Register children with an insurer; their basic package has no premium under age 18.
  • After taking out insurance, register with a GP and check whether you may qualify for healthcare benefit.
