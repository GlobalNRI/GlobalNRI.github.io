Dutch A2 Exam: Step-by-Step Preparation

Learn what the Dutch A2 level tests, how to improve speaking and listening, and how to build a realistic study plan for everyday life in the Netherlands.

Why A2 level matters

For many expats, Dutch A2 is the first major milestone after arrival. It is the level at which you can handle routine conversations, ask for basic help, understand public information, and manage everyday life without feeling lost in daily situations.

In practice, A2 means you can speak in short, simple sentences, understand common phrases, ask for directions, handle basic banking or admin tasks, and follow practical information in shops, on the phone, or in local offices.

Listening

Understand slow, clear speech about travel, work, shopping, appointments, and daily routine.

Reading

Read short texts, signs, simple forms, instructions, and basic emails or messages.

Writing

Write short messages, fill out forms, and describe everyday activities in simple Dutch.

Speaking

Introduce yourself, talk about your routine, ask for information, and respond in simple conversations.

KNM: Knowledge of Dutch Society

KNM is a separate computer-based part of the civic integration exam. It checks whether you understand how everyday systems work in the Netherlands, rather than testing your Dutch grammar.

Topics to study

  • Housing, contracts, neighbourhoods and local rules
  • Healthcare, the GP, emergency care and health insurance
  • Work, income, taxes and employee responsibilities
  • Education, childcare and parenting
  • Government, municipality, voting and important documents

Format and pass result

  • 45 multiple-choice questions on a computer
  • 45 minutes to complete the exam
  • The official pass score is set by DUO and can change with the exam version
  • Expect the result within eight weeks; the official result letter is the final confirmation

Best preparation: Study the official KNM learning materials, practise interpreting short everyday situations, and learn why an action is correct. Memorising isolated vocabulary is less useful than understanding the system behind it.

A realistic 8-week preparation plan

  1. Weeks 1–2: Build the core grammar and daily vocabulary. Focus on greetings, numbers, family, time, work, transport, and food.
  2. Weeks 3–4: Practise listening and speaking every day. Listen to short Dutch audio clips and repeat phrases aloud.
  3. Weeks 5–6: Work on reading and writing. Read short messages, fill forms, and write 4–5 short paragraphs about your day.
  4. Weeks 7–8: Do practice tests under exam conditions. Time your speaking and writing tasks and review errors carefully.

Best study habits

  • Learn phrases instead of only isolated words.
  • Repeat simple dialogues out loud daily.
  • Make a short list of tricky grammar points such as articles, verb order, and question forms.
  • Practice practical scenarios: doctor, bank, landlord, train station, school, and work.

Everyday Dutch topics that matter most

Life admin

Appointments, bank questions, BSN, rent, health insurance, and forms.

Travel & transport

Train tickets, asking for directions, travel times, and buying public transport passes.

Work & study

Talking about schedules, shifts, tasks, meetings, and basic workplace communication.

Home & family

Shopping, family members, routines, chores, and simple descriptions of your home.

Final checklist before the exam

  • Practice short real-life dialogues out loud.
  • Review common verb patterns: present tense, questions, negation, and time expressions.
  • Keep a vocabulary notebook for daily life and admin situations.
  • Take one or two mock tests before the exam day.
  • Stay calm and focus on communication rather than perfect grammar.
